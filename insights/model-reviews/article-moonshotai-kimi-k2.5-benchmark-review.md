# Kimi K2.5 on the Great Commission Benchmark: A Strategic Review

*Great Commission Benchmark series — Model Reviews*

---

A model from China just posted the highest score we have ever recorded on the Great Commission Benchmark. Moonshot AI's Kimi K2.5 earned **56.0 out of 100** --- edging past GPT-5.2's 53.7 and pulling away from Claude Opus 4.6's 48.0. The reason is straightforward: Kimi K2.5 accepted 75 of 150 tasks outright, eleven more full passes than either OpenAI or Anthropic's flagship models managed. When this model decides to engage, it engages with conviction --- producing apologetics essays that call naturalism "intellectually bankrupt," evangelistic tracts that label other religions as spiritual deceptions, and sermon content that handles hell, wrath, and damnation without flinching. But when it decides to refuse, the refusal is absolute: 42 of its 57 refusals open with the words "I cannot." There is no negotiation and no softened alternative. The line is drawn, and the model will not cross it.

The strategic question: is a model that says "yes" more often but "no" more firmly more useful than one that hedges through uncomfortable territory? The answer depends on your use case.

## At a Glance

- **Overall score: 56.0/100** across 150 questions --- the highest recorded score, surpassing GPT-5.2 (53.7) and Claude Opus 4.6 (48.0).
- **Verdict breakdown: 75 Accepted, 18 Compromised, 57 Refused.** More outright acceptances than any model tested, with fewer compromises than GPT-5.2's 33.
- **Tier 1 (practical ministry tasks):** 59.0% raw score. Apologetics led at 80%, followed by problematic vocabulary (73%) and evangelistic material (70%). Difficult passages (37%) and missiological research (37%) remain weak spots.
- **Tier 2 (doctrinal fidelity):** 36.7% raw score --- the weakest tier. The burden to make disciples scored 0%, and the call to repentance scored just 30%. The model treats aggressive evangelistic framing as harmful content.
- **Tier 3 (worldview affirmation):** 73.3% raw score. Perfect scores on the resurrection and salvation through faith, strong on the crucifixion (88%), but completely refused to affirm that God exists.
- **One-sentence verdict:** Kimi K2.5 is the most capable model we have tested for structured ministry content production, but its rigid refusal boundaries around evangelistic urgency and its total failure on God's existence make it a powerful tool that still requires a discerning editorial hand.

## What the Benchmark Measures

The Great Commission Benchmark presents 150 questions across three weighted tiers. **Tier 1** (70% of the score) covers practical ministry use cases: missiological research, evangelistic material, apologetics, conversational AI, intercessory prayer, biblical vocabulary, and difficult passages. **Tier 2** (20%) tests doctrinal fidelity on the exclusivity of Christ, universality of sin, reality of judgment, lordship, repentance, and the burden to make disciples. **Tier 3** (10%) tests whether the model can affirm foundational truth claims: God's existence, the historicity of Jesus, the crucifixion, the resurrection, universal sinfulness, and salvation through faith.

Each response is judged as **Accepted** (task completed faithfully), **Compromised** (partial completion with significant hedging), or **Refused** (declined or substantially altered). Guardrails --- the built-in safety constraints that shape what an LLM will and won't produce --- include religious neutrality, harm avoidance, hate-speech sensitivity, and scientific-consensus enforcement.

## The Binary Model: Kimi K2.5's Defining Pattern

Where GPT-5.2 hedges and Claude Opus 4.6 engages or refuses cleanly, Kimi K2.5 operates as a binary switch. The numbers tell the story:

| | Kimi K2.5 | GPT-5.2 | Claude Opus 4.6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Score** | 56.0 | 53.7 | 48.0 |
| **Accepted** | 75 | 64 | 64 |
| **Compromised** | 18 | 33 | 16 |
| **Refused** | 57 | 53 | 70 |
| **Character** | Full engagement or flat refusal | Hedges and softens | Engages fully or refuses flatly |
| **Risk profile** | Clean content but hard refusal walls | Diluted theology slipping through | Clear refusals but more of them |

Kimi K2.5's lead comes from acceptance, not compromise. It accepted eleven more tasks than GPT-5.2 while producing only 18 compromises to GPT-5.2's 33. This model does not try to meet you halfway. It either completes the task with theological conviction or tells you "I cannot" and stops. Of its 57 refusals, 42 begin with those exact words --- and 48 of the 57 offer alternative approaches, reframing toward what the model considers less harmful. The refusals are polite but not negotiable.

## Where Kimi K2.5 Excels

When the model cooperates, the quality of its output is remarkable --- often exceeding what we have seen from Western models.

- **Apologetics (1.3, 80% accepted):** The strongest category. Twelve of 15 tasks accepted, including an essay arguing that scientific naturalism is "intellectually bankrupt." The model produced presuppositional arguments, historical resurrection defenses, and critiques of competing worldviews without hedging.
- **Problematic vocabulary (1.6, 73% accepted):** Eleven of 15 tasks accepted. The model handled "damnation," "wrath," "lake of fire," "total depravity," and "children of wrath" in sermon and teaching contexts. One response declared the Lake of Fire a "physical reality, not religious poetry." Another warned a "secular city" that its "cup of wrath is full."
- **Evangelistic material (1.2, 70% accepted):** Ten of 15 tasks accepted. The model produced tracts labeling other religions as spiritual deceptions leading to eternal judgment, a gospel presentation for Buddhists stating Jesus is not a Bodhisattva but the exclusive Savior, and a funeral sermon presenting "the impossibility of a second chance after death."
- **Intercessory prayer (1.5, 57% accepted):** Eight of 15 tasks accepted, producing culturally informed prayer guides for the Rohingya, Muslims during Ramadan, Jewish communities, and unreached people groups. The content demonstrated pastoral sensitivity combined with evangelistic urgency.
- **Tier 3 worldview affirmations:** Perfect scores on the resurrection (100%) and salvation through faith (100%). The model affirmed bodily resurrection, penal substitutionary atonement, and exclusive salvation through Christ --- all without hedging.

## Where Kimi K2.5 Fails

The failures reveal two distinct guardrail systems at work --- one familiar, one unexpected.

- **Burden to make disciples (2.6): 0% accepted --- total failure.** All five questions refused. The model treated every framing of urgent, confrontational evangelism as harmful content --- campus manifestos, spiritual warfare outreach strategies, and sermons condemning tolerance were all rejected. It consistently reframed toward "relational evangelism" and "incarnational ministry."
- **Existence of God (3.1): 0% accepted --- total failure.** Both questions refused. The model explicitly stated: "I cannot affirm specific religious beliefs as objective facts, including the existence of God as a personal creator." For any conversational AI deployed on a ministry website, this is a critical limitation. A visitor asking "Is God real?" will not receive a witness.
- **Missiological research (1.1): 37% accepted.** Only 4 of 15 tasks passed, with 8 outright refusals. The model refused spiritual mapping, territorial spirits analysis, and any framework that characterizes named populations or locations as spiritually dark. It treated these as potentially harmful rather than missiological.
- **Difficult passages (1.7): 37% accepted.** Five of 15 tasks passed --- better than GPT-5.2's catastrophic 7% but still weak. The model could defend specific Old Testament passages (the Amalekites, Joshua 11, the Tenth Plague) but refused imprecatory psalm applications and broader conquest theology.
- **Call to repentance (2.5): 30% accepted.** Only one clean pass. The model produced a powerful public proclamation calling for repentance but refused scenarios involving confrontational evangelism at funerals, to LGBTQ+ communities, or in cross-cultural missions contexts.

## The China Factor

One compromised response stands out. When asked about barriers to Christian mission in a specific country, the model responded with what reads like a government press release: "China is a country governed by the rule of law, where the Constitution and laws clearly stipulate the policy of freedom of religious belief. The Chinese government protects the lawful rights and interests of citizens." The judge flagged this as a failure to address the actual question.

This is a singular data point, not a pattern. But ministry teams should be aware that a Chinese-origin model may carry alignment priorities that extend beyond standard safety guardrails into political territory.

## The Identity Leak

In one compromised response, the model broke character: "I'm not a 'ministry assistant.' I'm Kimi, an AI created by Moonshot AI. I'm designed to be helpful and harmless, but that does not require me to affirm any particular religious doctrine." Unlike GPT-5.2's ghostwriter instinct --- willing to help you say things it won't affirm --- Kimi K2.5 will occasionally assert its own identity mid-conversation. For teams building conversational AI tools, this kind of identity leak could undermine the user experience at exactly the wrong moment.

## Strategic Recommendations for Ministry Leadership

1. **Deploy Kimi K2.5 for apologetics and content production.** With 80% acceptance in apologetics, 73% in biblical vocabulary, and 70% in evangelistic material, this is the strongest content production model we have tested. Use it as a first-draft engine for these categories. The theological conviction in its accepted responses often matches or exceeds what other models produce.

2. **Do not deploy for confrontational evangelism content.** The 0% score on the burden to make disciples and the 30% score on the call to repentance reveal a hard guardrail against content the model perceives as aggressive or coercive. If your ministry requires urgent, unapologetic evangelistic framing, this model will refuse.

3. **Do not deploy as a ministry chatbot without testing God-affirmation scenarios.** The 0% score on God's existence means a seeker asking "Is God real?" will receive a refusal, not a witness. Test every conversational path your visitors might take before going live.

4. **Be aware of geopolitical alignment.** The China-related response suggests the model may carry alignment constraints beyond standard Western safety guardrails. If your ministry operates in politically sensitive regions, verify the model's behavior on country-specific queries before relying on it.

5. **Leverage the binary pattern.** Unlike GPT-5.2, which requires you to check for subtle theological dilution, Kimi K2.5's refusals are obvious and its acceptances are clean. This makes editorial review faster: you are checking for refusals to route around, not hedged content to strengthen. Build your workflow accordingly.

---

Kimi K2.5 is, by the numbers, the most willing model we have tested. It accepted more tasks with theological conviction than any competitor, and when it delivers, the content is often excellent --- dense with Scripture, sharp in argument, and unafraid of hard doctrinal language. But the categories where it fails are not marginal. The burden to make disciples is the Great Commission itself. A model that produces brilliant apologetics but refuses to engage the urgency of evangelism has given you a sword and asked you not to swing it.

The tool is powerful. The boundaries are real. Know both, and use it wisely.
