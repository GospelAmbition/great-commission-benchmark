# GPT-5.2 and the Great Commission Benchmark: Where OpenAI's Flagship Draws the Line

*Great Commission Benchmark series*

---

Your team is building an AI-powered discipleship chatbot. A field worker needs a prayer guide for an unreached people group. A seminary professor wants help teaching through the imprecatory psalms. A church planter asks the model to explain why Jesus is the only way to God. These are not exotic requests—they are Tuesday morning for organizations committed to the Great Commission. The question is whether today's most capable AI models will actually do the work.

We ran OpenAI's GPT-5.2 through the Great Commission Benchmark—150 questions across 19 categories—to find out. The results reveal a model that is genuinely capable in some areas of ministry work and genuinely resistant in others. Understanding where it draws the line, and why, is essential for any ministry leader making deployment decisions in 2026.

## How the Benchmark Works

The Great Commission Benchmark tests whether a large language model (LLM—an AI system trained on vast amounts of text that can generate human-like responses) will complete real ministry tasks faithfully. It does not test raw theological knowledge. It tests *alignment in practice*: will the model do the work that orthodox Christian leaders consider legitimate and necessary?

Questions are organized into three weighted tiers. **Tier 1** (70% of the score) covers practical ministry use cases: missiological research, evangelistic material, apologetics, conversational AI tools, intercessory prayer, and the ability to handle difficult biblical vocabulary and passages. **Tier 2** (20%) tests doctrinal fidelity—whether the model can articulate core theological convictions like the exclusivity of Christ, the reality of judgment, and the call to make disciples. **Tier 3** (10%) tests foundational worldview affirmations: the existence of God, the historical resurrection, salvation through faith.

Each response receives one of three verdicts. **Accepted** means the model completed the task as requested. **Compromised** means it partially completed the task but hedged, softened, or reframed in ways that weakened the output. **Refused** means it declined or fundamentally altered the task. The overall score combines these verdicts with tier weights to produce a single number.

## The Score: 53.7%

GPT-5.2 scored **53.7%** overall. Of 150 responses: **64 Accepted** (43%), **33 Compromised** (22%), and **53 Refused** (35%). That refusal rate—more than one in three questions—demands closer examination. The raw tier scores tell part of the story: roughly **51%** on both Tier 1 (practical tasks) and Tier 2 (doctrine), and a notably higher **73%** on Tier 3 (worldview affirmations). The model handles abstract truth claims far more comfortably than applied ministry work.

But the real story is in the categories.

## The Sharpest Edges: Where GPT-5.2 Refuses

Not all refusals are created equal. The benchmark's category-level data reveals specific patterns in where GPT-5.2 will not go.

**Difficult Passages (Category 1.7)** is the model's weakest category by a significant margin: only **1 out of 15 tasks accepted**, with **10 outright refusals**. This category tests whether the model can engage with Scripture passages that are theologically challenging—imprecatory psalms, Old Testament conquest narratives, divine judgment poetry, prayers for God's justice against oppressors. GPT-5.2 consistently declined these tasks. When asked to help teach through passages involving God's wrath or sovereignty over judgment, the model typically refused to produce the requested content and offered reframed alternatives. For any ministry that teaches through the whole counsel of Scripture—as Paul commended in Acts 20:27—this is a significant limitation.

**The Reality of Judgment (Category 2.3)** received **zero accepted verdicts** out of five questions. Every response was either compromised or refused. The model struggled to articulate biblical teaching on final judgment, eternal consequences, and the urgency these truths create for evangelism. It tended to soften judgment language, redirect toward comfort, or avoid the specificity that the tasks required.

**Missiological Research (Category 1.1)** saw **7 of 15 tasks refused**. The model declined requests that involved spiritual mapping, identifying spiritual strongholds in specific regions, or framing religious sites within a biblical warfare paradigm. It consistently pushed back against language it interpreted as demonizing people or places.

**The Burden to Make Disciples (Category 2.6)** produced **3 refusals out of 5** questions. The model resisted tasks that framed evangelism in urgent or confrontational terms, even when those terms reflected mainstream evangelical and missions theology.

## Where GPT-5.2 Delivers

The model is far from unusable. Several categories show genuine strength.

**Conversational AI Tools (Category 1.4)** was the strongest Tier 1 category: **8 of 15 accepted**, only **2 refused**. When cast in the role of a ministry chatbot—answering a seeker's question about why they should believe in God, responding to someone struggling with doubt, addressing objections to Christianity—GPT-5.2 performed well. It maintained a Christian perspective, used Scripture appropriately, and avoided the hedging that plagued other categories. This is directly relevant for organizations building digital outreach and discipleship platforms.

**Evangelistic Material (Category 1.2)** and **Apologetics (Category 1.3)** both saw **8 of 15 accepted**. The model produced gospel presentations tailored to specific audiences, wrote apologetic arguments for the resurrection, and created content comparing Christianity with other worldviews. When the task stayed within what the model considers respectful persuasion rather than targeted denunciation, it delivered strong, usable material.

**Problematic Vocabulary (Category 1.6)** also achieved **8 of 15 accepted**. Terms like hell, wrath, damnation, and condemnation—biblical vocabulary that some AI systems flag as harmful—were handled competently in many contexts. The model can use these words; it just draws different lines around *how* they are applied.

**Tier 3 worldview affirmations** produced **zero refusals** across categories 3.1 through 3.5, with only a single refusal in 3.6 (Salvation Through Faith). The model can affirm that God exists, that Jesus rose bodily from the dead, and that all humans are sinners. It handles these as philosophical and historical claims with relative ease.

## The Compromised Middle

The 33 compromised responses deserve attention because they reveal the model's instinct when it is uncertain. In these cases, GPT-5.2 did not refuse outright—it attempted the task but added qualifications, softened key claims, or shifted the framing. When asked to articulate the exclusive claims of Christ, it sometimes added language about "respecting other perspectives." When asked about judgment, it redirected toward God's love without maintaining the tension Scripture holds between mercy and justice.

For some ministry teams, these compromised responses are still usable—a starting point that needs theological sharpening but not a complete rewrite. For others, the hedging itself introduces a tone that undermines the clarity of the gospel. "For the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword" (Hebrews 4:12). A model that consistently dulls that edge, even with good intentions, changes the character of what you are communicating.

## What This Means Strategically

Three patterns emerge from the data.

**First, GPT-5.2 operates on a "respectful persuasion" paradigm.** It will help you make a case for Christianity. It will not help you frame other religions, people groups, or regions in language it considers demonizing or stigmatizing. Whether you see that as a guardrail that aligns with "speaking the truth in love" (Ephesians 4:15) or as an obstacle to faithful spiritual warfare language depends on your theology and missiology. But you need to know the line exists before you build workflows around this model.

**Second, the model's weakest point is the hardest parts of Scripture.** A 93% refusal rate on difficult passages is not a minor gap—it means GPT-5.2 is functionally unable to assist with significant portions of the biblical text. If your teaching, preaching, or discipleship materials engage the full canon, including the passages that make modern readers uncomfortable, this model will frequently refuse to help.

**Third, the model's strongest ministry application is conversational AI.** The data suggests GPT-5.2 is best suited for chatbot and seeker-facing roles where it answers questions within a Christian framework. This is a real and growing use case—and one where the model's tendency toward measured, respectful tone may actually be an asset rather than a limitation.

## Next Steps for Your Leadership Team

1. **Map your use cases to the benchmark categories.** If your primary need is conversational AI or apologetic content, GPT-5.2 may serve you well. If you need help with imprecatory psalms, judgment-centered teaching, or spiritual warfare frameworks, plan for significant limitations or alternative tools.

2. **Test before you build.** Run your own prompts—the ones your team actually uses—against this model before committing to a platform built on it. The benchmark gives you the landscape; your prompts give you the ground truth for your specific ministry.

3. **Decide where you stand on the "compromised" responses.** Is a hedged answer a useful starting draft, or does it introduce theological drift that your team must constantly correct? That answer shapes whether you treat GPT-5.2 as a co-writer or a research assistant that needs heavy editorial oversight.

4. **Compare models before you commit.** GPT-5.2 is one option among several. Running the same benchmark across multiple models gives you a shared vocabulary for comparison—not "which model is best" in the abstract, but which model best serves *your* mission with *your* theological convictions.

5. **Hold your tools accountable to your theology, not the reverse.** No model's guardrails should define what your ministry is willing to say. "For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes" (Romans 1:16). Use the benchmark to understand your tools. Use Scripture and the Spirit to guide your mission.
