# Great Commission Benchmark: do not write like this

Applies to **all public GCB copy**: model-review articles, other blog posts, newsletter emails, and Highlight emails.

These are common AI-writing formulas. None of them proves a text is generated. Humans use them too. The problem is a dense mix: canned framing, three-part lists, vague abstractions, perfect structure, excessive transitions, and few concrete details. Do not use these patterns.

## Formulas and phrases to avoid

1. “It’s not just X—it’s Y.” (“This isn’t just a tool—it’s a revolution.”)
2. “Whether you’re X, Y, or Z…”
3. “From X to Y…” (“From streamlining workflows to unlocking creativity…”)
4. “In today’s fast-paced/digital/ever-evolving world…”
5. “At its core…”
6. “Let’s dive in.” / “Let’s explore.”
7. “Here’s the thing:”
8. “The bottom line?” / “The best part?”
9. “But that’s not all.”
10. “Imagine a world where…”
11. “Picture this:”
12. “Enter: [product or concept].”
13. “Meet [product], your new…”
14. “Gone are the days of…”
15. “Say goodbye to X and hello to Y.”
16. “X is more than just Y.”
17. “Not only X, but also Y.” (especially when repeated)
18. The rule of three: “faster, smarter, and more efficient.”
19. Repeated contrast pairs: “Not this, but that”; “less X, more Y.”
20. “By leveraging/harnessing the power of…”
21. “Unlock the power/potential of…”
22. “Seamlessly integrates…”
23. “Empowers users to…”
24. “Streamline, optimize, and enhance…”
25. “A game-changer.”
26. “A testament to…”
27. “A beacon of hope/innovation/progress.”
28. “A tapestry of…”
29. “Delve into…”
30. “Navigate the complexities/landscape of…”
31. “The ever-evolving landscape of…”
32. “Paves the way for…”
33. “Marks a significant step/milestone…”
34. “Underscores the importance of…”
35. “Serves as a reminder that…”
36. “Ultimately, the future of X lies in…”
37. “As we move forward…”
38. “Only time will tell.”

## Structure and voice to avoid

39. Perfectly symmetrical paragraphs (claim, one explanation, miniature conclusion).
40. Excessive headings that chop simple material (“Understanding X,” “Why It Matters,” “Key Benefits,” “The Road Ahead”).
41. Every heading in Title Case when combined with many short sections. Prefer sentence-case section heads that name the content.
42. A summary that only repeats the introduction.
43. Constant signposting: First, Additionally, Moreover, Furthermore, However, In conclusion.
44. Forced comprehensiveness (benefits, challenges, applications, implications, best practices, future trends) when the piece is narrow.
45. Balanced-but-empty caveats: “While X offers many benefits, it is important to consider the potential challenges.”
46. Generic nuance: “There is no one-size-fits-all solution.” “The answer depends on your specific needs.”
47. Unnecessary definitions of familiar terms.
48. Overuse of em dashes for dramatic contrast.
49. Overuse of parenthetical clarifications of points already clear.
50. Polished but low-information sentences: “By embracing innovation and fostering collaboration, organizations can position themselves for long-term success.”
51. Abstract nouns instead of concrete actions.
52. Unsupported universal claims: “Businesses of all sizes are increasingly turning to AI…”
53. Invented consensus: “Experts agree,” “Studies show,” “It is widely recognized” with no source.
54. Generic “For instance” examples that add no evidence.
55. Uniformly upbeat tone (every challenge is an “opportunity,” every change is “exciting”).
56. No genuine preference or sharp judgment.
57. Impersonal advice that ends with a motivational closer (“Embrace the journey.”).
58. Restating the prompt or question before answering.
59. Meta-description of the piece: “In this article, we’ll explore…” “This comprehensive guide will cover…”
60. A closing offer: “Let me know if you’d like me to expand on any of these points.”
61. Review titles that pair the top and bottom categories: “Strong on X, Weak on Y.” Name the behavior instead (refusals, hedging, what the model would or would not say).

Write like a peer with a specific finding. Name the model, the score, the category, and what a ministry person should do. Prefer concrete verbs over atmosphere.
